The Market Is Too Sure the Fed Hikes in December

Futures price roughly a 90% chance that the Fed raises rates again on 9 December. I think a pause is far more likely than 1-in-10: jobs growth has stalled, housing is weakening, and much of the inflation jump is an energy shock the Fed can't fix with higher rates. I express that view with a small, capped-risk bet that pays if the Fed holds.

Section 1The starting point
What the Market Thinks

Before having a view, you need to know what's already in the price. Right now, the market is close to certain the Fed hikes in December.

The see-saw: price = 100 − rate

A SOFR future is quoted as 100 minus the interest rate people expect over a three-month window. If traders expect 4.18%, the future trades at 95.82. Rate and price move like two ends of a see-saw: if expected rates fall, the price rises. One basis point (0.01%) is worth $25 per contract.

Reading the price
Overnight SOFR is 3.88% but the December future implies 4.18%. That extra ≈30 bp is the market saying: one more 25 bp hike in December, plus a little chance of more early next year. If the Fed holds instead, that expected rate has to fall, and the future has to rise.
Section 2Why I disagree
Why a Pause Is Underpriced

My case rests on data, not on guessing what any politician wants. Here's the evidence for a hold, and the evidence against me, side by side.

1
Jobs growth has stalled
September payrolls rose by just 29,000, far below forecasts, and unemployment rose to 4.2%. Hiking into a weakening jobs market risks tipping it over.
2
The inflation jump is mostly energy
Headline CPI hit 3.7%, but gasoline (+34% year on year) did much of the work. Core CPI is 2.6%. Higher rates can't produce more oil, and central banks often look through supply shocks like this.
3
Housing is already cracking
September housing starts fell and pending home sales dropped to a record low. The rate-sensitive parts of the economy are already slowing.
4
The Fed hasn't committed
Chair Kevin Warsh declined to say when, or whether, further hikes would come, and senior officials have sounded more cautious since September. A 90% price leaves no room for that doubt.
The case against me
Argument for a hikeMy response
September minutes said another hike would likely be appropriate by year-endThat was before the weak jobs and housing data. The minutes also said decisions depend on incoming data.
Headline inflation is rising, not falling (3.4% → 3.7%)True, and it's the biggest risk to this trade. But it's energy-led, and core rose only modestly.
Other central banks are hiking (the ECB raised rates)Each bank sets policy for its own economy; US jobs data is weaker.
The economy has otherwise been strongYes, which is why I put a hold at ~30%, not 50%+. I'm arguing it's underpriced, not that it's the most likely outcome.
Background, not a reason
The US midterm elections on 3 November add political noise around rates. I don't rely on it: the Fed is independent, and the December meeting comes a month after the vote. If anything, the Fed will want any decision to look purely data-driven.
Section 3Structure · strikes · payoff
Turning the View Into a Trade

I want something that pays well if the Fed holds, costs little if I'm wrong, and can't lose more than I put in. A bull call spread on the December SOFR future does all three.

Why SR3Z6, and why these dates line up

SR3Z6's options expire on 11 December, just two days after the Fed's decision on 9 December, so the result is fully in the price before my options settle. The future itself prices the average rate from 16 December to mid-March, a window that includes the next (late-January) meeting. So a hold only helps fully if the market doesn't immediately price a January hike instead.

Why these strikes

I worked out where the future should trade on 11 December under five outcomes, by averaging the expected overnight rate across the 91-day window. The 95.875 / 96.00 strikes sit neatly between the "hike" and "hold" clusters.

The design choice
Even in the least helpful hold case — the Fed pauses but everyone expects a January hike — the spread still pays 11.3 of a possible 12.5 bp. That's deliberate: the trade should reward being right about December without needing me to also be right about January.

Section 5How I got the numbers
Method, Limits and Sources
Scenario prices

The future settles on the average SOFR over 16 Dec 2026 – 17 Mar 2027 (91 days). For each outcome, I take SOFR's current position in the Fed's range (3.88%), shift it by the December decision, and add any January hike in proportion to how much of the window comes after a late-January meeting (about 48 of 91 days):

def sr3z6_price(sofr_after_dec, p_jan_hike): avg_rate = sofr_after_dec + 0.25 * p_jan_hike * 48 / 91 return 100 - avg_rate # e.g. hold, January hike fully priced: sr3z6_price(3.88, 1.0) # -> 95.988 -> spread pays 11.3 bp
Why I set an entry limit instead of a model price

A standard bell-curve options model (Bachelier, with an assumed 50–90 bp yearly volatility) values this spread at 3.3–4.5 bp. Weighting my scenario payoffs by the market's own 90/10 odds gives about 1.3 bp. They disagree because a Fed meeting doesn't produce one smooth range of outcomes: it produces two clusters, "hike" and "hold". So I don't trust either number. I set a limit of 3 bp, where the trade still works at my odds, and check a live quote before trading.

Limits of this memo
Prices are snapshots from 22–23 October 2026 and will move. The late-January meeting date is an assumption. Option premiums are estimates, not live quotes. This is a paper trade written for learning.
